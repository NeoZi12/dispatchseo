---

On DispatchSEO Cloud, a new site runs on autopilot. You don't connect an AI
of your own. Our server picks the keywords, writes the articles and
publishes them on a fixed weekly schedule. This page explains what it
does, when it does it, and what you can change.

If you run DispatchSEO yourself (self-hosted), your sites still use your
own coding agent, and nothing on this page applies to them.

## Before the first article

Once you finish setup, three things happen without you:

1. We read your site. The server visits up to 150 of your pages and
   notes your sections, page titles and the pages you link to most.
2. We describe your site. From that reading and your homepage, a model
   writes a short profile: a tagline, a one-line description, a longer
   description and the categories you cover. You can rewrite any of it on
   **Settings > Brand**. Once you save your own version, we never overwrite
   it.
3. We research keywords. The server looks for searches people make
   around your categories (and, once Google Search Console has data, around
   the searches you already show up for). It keeps up to six ideas queued.

The first article usually starts the next morning. If we can't read your
homepage (some sites block servers, or sit behind a login), the dashboard
asks you to describe your site on **Settings > Site** instead, and the
first article starts from your description.

Every article publishes the moment it passes the quality gate. There's no
waiting for a publishing hour, and nothing for you to approve first.

## How topics are picked

You never approve ideas. Each keyword has to pass every check below, in
this order:

- It gets at least 50 searches a month.
- Its difficulty is under a ceiling set by your site's authority. A new
  site gets 10 out of 100, and the ceiling rises as your site earns links.
- It's in the language your site is set to.
- It isn't on your list of [topics to avoid](#what-you-control).
- You don't already have it. That covers anything you've published,
  queued or written about before, near-duplicates included: "how to choose a
  crm" and "how to choose crm software" count as one article.
- It fits your site. A judging model asks whether your readers would search
  it, whether your site can answer it credibly, and whether the searcher
  wants to learn or compare rather than buy right now. Topics that won't
  still be searched in a year are skipped too.

The ones left are ranked by how much traffic they can realistically bring
(searches a month, discounted by difficulty), and the best go to the front
of the queue. Research runs again once a week.

If nothing passes, research widens once: five more points of difficulty
and at least 20 searches a month instead of 50. If that still finds
nothing, the dashboard says "No winnable keywords found yet" and research
tries again the next week. Suggesting a topic yourself is the fastest way
past that.

## How often it publishes

| Plan | Articles a week |
|---|---|
| Starter (and the free trial) | 1 |
| Growth | 3 |
| Scale | 5 |

The week resets on Monday (UTC). Up to three a week, two articles for the
same site never start less than two days apart, so three a week lands
roughly Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Five a week is one a day - never two
in one morning. The week resets on Monday, so a site that starts mid-week
writes until Sunday and then its full five. The dashboard shows the date of
the next one.

There is no daily option, on purpose. A young site that suddenly publishes
every day looks like a content farm to Google, and a steady pace does
better.

An article that stops because of a problem on our side (a supplier
outage, our keyword data budget for the day) doesn't count against your
week, and the next try isn't held back by the spacing.

## How each article is written

For every article the server reads the pages that rank on page one for
the keyword and pulls out facts with the exact quote behind each one. It
plans an article that covers what searchers expect and adds one thing
those pages don't have. Then it writes from that research only, checks
every figure and claim against its source, and checks the result isn't too
close to any page-one article or to your own earlier posts. An article that
can't beat page one isn't written at all.

Then it publishes the same way as before. You get a pull request on your
repo (merged for you on automatic publishing once its checks pass), a post
on your WordPress site, or a page on the blog we host at
yoursite.com/blog. An article only shows as **Live** once its page loads
on your site and shows the article itself. A pull request that is still
open shows as **Waiting for a merge**, with a link to it, and the
dashboard warns you if any article hasn't gone live within two days.

We start tracking an article's keyword on Google once the article is
live, and check rankings weekly.

### The hosted blog

If your repo has no blog folder, we host the blog for you and open one
pull request in your repo that forwards yoursite.com/blog to it. Until
that pull request is merged, the dashboard shows a **Merge the forwarding
pull request** card linking straight to it. Your first article goes out
either way; after that, new articles wait until /blog forwards, because a
page nobody can reach from your site does you no good. We re-check the
forwarding every week. If your site's host isn't one we can open a pull
request for, **Settings > Publishing** shows the one rule to add yourself.

If articles have nowhere to go yet, the dashboard asks you to **Choose
where articles go**, and nothing is written until you do.

## When it pauses itself

The autopilot stops writing if the numbers say more articles would not
help:

- **Search Console impressions collapse.** Your last 7 days get fewer than
  40% of the impressions the previous four weeks predict. This only counts
  on a site that had at least 500 impressions in those four weeks. A drop
  like that usually means a Google update or a manual action.
- **Google shows your new articles to nobody.** Your last three articles
  have been indexed for three weeks or more, and none of them has a single
  impression.

When it pauses, the dashboard tells you which one happened, and we email
you. Articles that were already written still publish. It doesn't start
again by itself. Look at Search Console first, then press **Resume
autopilot** on your dashboard's Home when you're ready.

## When something goes wrong

Home shows a short note when something needs a look: articles that keep
stopping, one that hasn't gone live in two days, keyword research that
can't run. Problems on our side say so and fix themselves; for the rest,
the note says what to do. A single blip doesn't show up at all.

You can also pause it yourself at any time from the dashboard.

## What you control

These settings are all you need to change. Everything else runs by itself.

- **Pause and resume.**
- **Brand.** Your tagline, short and long description, and categories. The
  writer uses the short description to decide what is on-topic, and
  research starts from the categories.
- **Tone.** House rules the writer follows on every article, for example
  "Write in British English" or "Never mention competitors by name". Up to
  2000 characters.
- **Topics to avoid.** Up to 50. A topic blocks every keyword that contains
  all of its words, so "crypto" blocks "best crypto wallets". Ideas already
  queued under a new blocked topic leave the queue straight away.
- **Suggest a topic.** Type a search phrase. It goes through the same
  checks as research (on-topic, not blocked, not a duplicate) and is either
  queued or turned down with the reason.
- **Author.** A name, a short bio and a page on your own site. With a name,
  articles list that person as the author. Without one, the author is your
  site.
- **Your blog.** Where articles go and how the hosted blog looks.
- **Google Search Console** and **billing.**

Every one of these is also an MCP tool (`get_autopilot`, `pause_writer`,
`set_tone` and so on, listed under [MCP tools](/docs/mcp-tools#autopilot)),
for anyone who would rather ask an AI to change them.
