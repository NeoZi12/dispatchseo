SEO for a SaaS is a 12-month job with four phases: set up the basics in months 1-2, expect almost nothing in month 3, earn your first clicks in months 4-7, and let older pages carry the load after that. The work that matters is the same every week - pick a buyer-intent topic, [publish one good page](/blog/content-marketing-for-saas), read Search Console - and the founders who get results are the ones who keep it going through the quiet months.

> **TL;DR** - Months 1-2: Search Console, sitemap, 5-10 pages on what buyers search. Month 3: first impressions, almost no clicks. Months 4-7: commercial topics, one page a day at most, rewrite titles that get views but no clicks. Months 8-12: refresh winners and link pages together. Automate the research and drafting; keep the decisions. Hire only when the bottleneck is judgment, not hours.

## Why does a SaaS need a different SEO plan than a shop or a blog?

A SaaS buyer searches in a pattern a shop's customer doesn't. They look for the problem ("how do I track rankings"), then for categories ("rank tracker for small sites"), then for comparisons, and only then for your name. Each stage is a different page, and the sales cycle is long enough that the visitor who reads your guide today signs up three weeks later.

That shapes the plan in two ways. You write for the buyer's problem before you write for your product, and you accept that early pages measure in impressions, not signups. ## What do months 1 and 2 actually involve?

Foundation, and it is shorter than it sounds:

1. Verify the domain in [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console) and submit the sitemap. Nothing else in this plan works if you can't see what Google reports.
2. Write down the three problems your product solves, in your buyers' words. These become your topic list.
3. Publish 5-10 pages: one per problem, plus two or three on the categories a buyer compares you in.
4. Check each title and H1 before shipping. A free [title length checker](/free-tools/seo-title-length-checker) catches titles that get cut off.

Skip the 200-point audit and the backlink campaign. A site with ten pages has nothing in an audit to act on. [Best SEO tools for beginners](/blog/best-seo-tools-for-beginners) lists the four free tools that cover this stage.

## What should you expect to see in month 3?

Impressions at positions 30 to 80 and clicks you can count on your fingers. This is where most plans die, because the founder reads "no traffic" as "it's not working."

## Which keywords are worth writing in months 4 to 7?

Commercial and comparison topics first, mid-tail before head terms. A young domain can't win "SEO software," but it can win a specific question a buyer asks right before choosing.

Three filters, in order:

- **Intent.** Would someone with a budget type this? "Rank tracker for small sites" beats "what is a SERP."
- **Winnability.** Open page 1. If it is forums, thin posts and agency pitches, you can compete. If it is five household-name tools, skip it for now.
- **Fit.** You must be able to say something true and specific that page 1 lacks - your own numbers, a tested setup, a defended opinion.

A [keyword clustering tool](/free-tools/keyword-clustering-tool) helps once you have a list of 30 or more phrases and need to decide which belong on one page.

One caution on comparison and "alternative" pages. They fit the buyer's journey, which is why founders write ten of them in a week. Keep them to a small share of the plan and give each real first-hand detail.

## How often should you publish?

One good page a day is the ceiling, and one or two a week is a fine pace to start. Search engines don't punish publishing speed. They discount batches of pages that share the same structure with different keywords swapped in, so a pace you can keep to a quality bar beats a burst.

Before shipping any page, check three things: does it answer the query in its first paragraph, does it contain something not on page 1 (a number you measured, a config you ran), and does it differ in layout from your last few posts? Updating older pages counts as publishing. [Content freshness and SEO](/blog/content-freshness-seo) covers what a useful refresh changes.

## What happens in months 8 to 12?

The mix shifts from writing new pages to improving what already shows signs of life. Pull the pages sitting at positions 5 to 20 for their own keyword. These are the cheapest wins on the site: Google has already half-trusted them, and a sharper title, a better opening answer and one new section can move them onto page 1.

Also link in both directions. When you publish a new guide, add a link to it from two or three older, related pages by wrapping words already there. Then cluster new topics around the queries that are starting to rank, so each new page lands next to a page Google already trusts.

## When should you hire someone instead?

Hire when your bottleneck is judgment, not hours. That means: you can't tell which topics matter, you have budget for a specialist's retainer, or you need technical work on a large site (migrations, thousands of programmatic pages, international setups). A freelancer or agency is also the right call if you will never read a Search Console report.

Don't hire to get past the quiet months. An agency can't make month 3 arrive faster, and a founder who pays a retainer and then ignores the reports gets the same curve at a higher price.

## When is this plan the wrong fit?

If your product is brand new with no audience, paid ads or community posts will validate demand faster than a 12-month wait. If your market has no search volume - a category people don't yet know to search - SEO is the wrong first channel. And if you can't commit to roughly 12 months of steady work, the early effort is mostly wasted.

## FAQ

**How long does SEO take for a SaaS?**
Expect first impressions within 1-3 months and first meaningful clicks around months 4-7 for a new site without links. Competitive terms take longer, and nothing here is guaranteed.

**How much should a SaaS startup spend on SEO?**
The core tools are free: Search Console, Google autocomplete and Keyword Planner. The real cost is time, a few hours a week by hand, less if you automate research and drafting.

**What keywords should a SaaS target first?**
Problem-and-category queries with commercial intent that a young domain can win, such as specific questions buyers ask before comparing tools. Check that page 1 is beatable before you write.

**Is SEO better than paid ads for a SaaS?**
They do different jobs. Ads give fast, paid signal; SEO is slow and then compounds. Many founders run ads to test demand and SEO to build a durable channel.

**Can I run SaaS SEO alone?**
Yes. One person can run this plan with a weekly Search Console check and a steady publishing habit. Automation helps with the research and drafting load.
