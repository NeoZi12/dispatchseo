A published article cost us a median of 6 cents in model spend, 10 cents on average, and 23 cents all-in once the runs that stopped before publishing are counted. Those are DispatchSEO's own production numbers for the week of 2026-11-01 to 2026-11-07: 21 attempts on two sites billed through the API, 10 articles published, $2.28 spent in total. It is a small sample from a young pipeline and is presented as ours, not as the cost of AI articles in general. The interesting part is not the model bill, which is a rounding error, but where the other half of the runs went.

> **TL;DR** - Model spend per published article: 6 cents median, 10 cents average, 24 cents at most, for a fact-checked article with a cover image and internal links. Of $2.28 spent, $1.27 went to the 11 runs that stopped before publishing. The research data that picks topics is a separate metered bill and is not in these numbers. Vendors charging $1 to $3 an article are paying for the pipeline, the failed runs and their margin, not the model.

Two more sites ran the same pipeline in the period on the owner's own AI subscription, where model usage is not billed per run; they are left out because their recorded cost is not comparable. The search-volume and difficulty data that decides what to write about is a separate, metered bill and is not in the table. Later articles ran through a batch API that charges half the standard rate, which is part of why the median sits below the average.

## Why did half the runs stop?

A stopped run is not always a failure of the tool. The 11 here split into two kinds. The first is the fact gate doing its job: the research step found a topic, the writer drafted, and the citation check could not ground the article in more than one source, so the pipeline parked it rather than publish something thin. The second is pipeline bugs in a pipeline that was weeks old; most were fixed within a day of appearing. We are not going to pretend the split was all gate refusals. The point is that a stopped run still costs money, 12 cents on average here, and that a pipeline that never stops is one that publishes everything it drafts.

## Where does the money go inside a run?

The draft and its rewrite passes are the money. Research is cheap because it is mostly calls to search-data providers, which bill separately. Fact filtering, section planning and the citation check are short prompts over text that already exists. The draft is the one long generation, and each rewrite pass is another one. Seven of the ten published articles went through once; three needed a second pass. The gate bounds that: an article that fails twice on the same problem is parked, not rewritten a third time.

## What does this mean when you price or buy a tool?

A vendor charging $1 to $3 an article is unlikely to be spending that on the model. They are paying for the orchestration, the search data, the publishing integrations, the failed runs, the support, and their margin, and that is a reasonable thing to charge for. It does mean that "unlimited AI words" is cheap to offer, because words are the cheap part.

For a buyer, the better question is not "how much per article" but "how many of your runs publish, and what stops the rest". A tool whose runs never stop has no gate. A tool that stops half its runs and tells you why is spending your money on not publishing, which is the part you are paying for.
