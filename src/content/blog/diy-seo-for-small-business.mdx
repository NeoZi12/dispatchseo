DIY SEO for a small business means handling technical setup, on-page tags, content, local listings, and Search Console monitoring yourself instead of paying an agency - and for a small local business that's a legitimate call: none of it requires code, most of it is free, and the checklist below covers what actually moves a ranking. What it can't tell you is which of those steps you do once and forget, and which ones come back every week whether you have time or not - that split is what separates a DIY project that works from one that quietly stops three months in.

> **TL;DR** - The DIY SEO checklist for a small business breaks into five jobs: technical basics (crawlability, sitemap, mobile), on-page (titles, meta descriptions, headings), local SEO (Google Business Profile, citations, reviews), content, and watching Search Console. Every item is free and doable without code. The part today's page 1 for this exact query doesn't say out loud: about a third of that checklist is a one-time setup task, and the rest comes back on a schedule for as long as the business wants to keep ranking - new content, rank checks, GSC review, fresh reviews. That recurring third is also the part most DIY projects quietly stop doing first.

## Technical basics: can Google actually crawl and index the site

Before anything else, Google's own [SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) puts crawlability first: can Google reach the page and the CSS/JS it depends on, is there a sitemap listing the URLs that matter, and does the URL Inspection tool in Search Console confirm Google actually sees what a visitor sees. A sitemap is optional in Google's own words - "focus first on making sure people know about your site" - but it costs nothing to add and removes one variable from every crawl question afterward.

## On-page: titles, meta descriptions, headers, internal links

Google's guidance here is specific enough to follow line for line: a title tag should be "unique to the page, clear and concise" and, for a business, include the business name and location where relevant. A meta description is a "succinct, one- or two-sentence summary" - short and unique per page, not padded to hit a length target. Headings can appear "out of order" without penalty, so don't worry about perfect H1-H2-H3 nesting; worry about whether they actually help someone scan the page. Images need real, descriptive alt text explaining "the relationship between the image and your content," not a keyword stuffed in for the crawler. ## Local SEO: the step most DIY checklists shortchange

Search the checklist term and most results give this one paragraph; for a business with a physical location or a service area, it's often the highest-leverage item on the whole list. Set up starts with claiming or adding a [Google Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9798848), then verifying it - Google currently offers a standard verification flow and a newer video-verification option for businesses that qualify for it. After that, the job is consistency: the same name, address, and phone number everywhere the business is listed, a handful of citations on directories relevant to the industry rather than generic ones, and reviews that keep arriving instead of stopping after the first push.

## Content and keyword research: the part with no finish line

Google's content guidance is blunt about what doesn't work: don't write for the crawler, don't "excessively repeat the same words," and don't copy what's already ranking. What does work is content that answers a real question a customer would type, kept current instead of frozen the day it was published. [The free keyword and SEO tooling stack](/blog/best-free-seo-tools) covers what's genuinely free to research with - Search Console's own query data, a free-tier keyword tool, a technical crawler - before a single dollar gets spent.

## Watching Google Search Console: what to check and how often

Verify the property, then check it on a cadence: which queries are earning impressions but not clicks (a title or description problem, usually), whether new pages actually got indexed, and whether a crawl error or manual action shows up before it costs a month of visibility. None of this is a one-time setup step - it's the monitoring half of the checklist, and it only pays off if someone keeps opening it.

## FAQ

**Can a small business really do all of this without hiring an agency?**
The technical, on-page, and local-setup items - yes, in an afternoon or two, no code required. The honest catch is the recurring third: new content, rank checks, and Search Console review don't have a finish line, and that's the part that stalls first once the business gets busy again.

**What's the single highest-leverage DIY step for a local business specifically?**
A verified, complete Google Business Profile with consistent name/address/phone across directories. It's the one item most generic DIY checklists give a single line to, and the one most directly tied to appearing in local search results at all.

**Do I need to submit a sitemap for Google to index my site?**
No - Google's own guidance calls it optional and focuses first on making sure the site is discoverable at all. It's still worth the ten minutes it takes, since it removes one variable from every future "why isn't this page indexed" question.

**How often should I actually check Google Search Console?**
Monthly is enough to catch a real problem before it costs meaningful traffic; weekly only pays off once there's enough content shipping regularly to make a weekly change worth reading. What matters is that someone is actually checking it, on some schedule, rather than never.

**What happens if I do all the one-time setup and then stop?**
The one-time work doesn't decay - the technical and on-page basics stay done. What stalls is everything the recurring column carries: no new content means no new rankings, and a Search Console nobody's watching won't tell anyone that on its own.

Every DIY SEO checklist for a small business ends up saying roughly the same true things: fix the technical basics, get the on-page tags right, claim the local listing, publish something, watch Search Console. None of it is wrong. What it leaves for the reader to notice on their own is that half of that list is a Tuesday afternoon and the other half is a standing weekly commitment - and knowing which is which, going in, is the difference between a DIY project that's still running in a year and one that quietly isn't.
